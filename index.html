Chapter 01

The History
of Formula One

Formula One is the pinnacle of motorsport — a world championship series born in 1950 that transformed from post-war racing into a global spectacle watched by 1.5 billion fans. Here's how it evolved.

1950
The Birth of the World Championship
The first-ever Formula One World Championship race was held at Silverstone, England on May 13, 1950. King George VI attended, and Giuseppe Farina of Alfa Romeo won. Farina went on to claim the first World Drivers' Championship that year. The sport had 6 races in its debut season — today it has 24.
1950s–60s
The Brave Era — Speed Without Safety
Early F1 was thrillingly dangerous. Drivers raced with minimal safety equipment on treacherous circuits with no barriers. Juan Manuel Fangio dominated the late 1950s, winning 5 championships. Jim Clark and Jack Brabham became heroes, but the era was marked by tragedy as fatalities were tragically common.
1970s
Ground Effect & the Safety Revolution
Lotus introduced "ground effect" aerodynamics — using the car's underside to generate massive downforce. This era also saw growing pressure to improve safety after the deaths of multiple drivers. Jackie Stewart became the most vocal advocate for safer circuits, and his campaign began changing the sport forever.
1980s
Turbo Warfare & Prost vs Senna
Turbocharged engines producing over 1,400 horsepower in qualifying trim made F1 feel like science fiction. The decade ended with the greatest rivalry in sports history: Ayrton Senna (Brazil) vs Alain Prost (France). Their battles at McLaren were legendary — passionate and occasionally violent on track.
1994
The Darkest Weekend
At the 1994 San Marino Grand Prix, Roland Ratzenberger died in qualifying and three-time World Champion Ayrton Senna was killed in the race. It was a devastating moment that permanently changed the FIA's approach to safety, leading to massive improvements in car design, barriers, and circuit safety that have made modern F1 remarkably safe.
2000s
The Schumacher Era
Michael Schumacher dominated with Ferrari, winning 5 consecutive championships from 2000–2004. He rewrote the record books and brought unprecedented professionalism — physical training, simulator work, and technical collaboration — to a driver's role. At his peak, his races were almost impossible to beat.
2010s
Hamilton & the Hybrid Era
New turbocharged hybrid engines arrived in 2014, and Mercedes dominated for 8 straight years. Lewis Hamilton became the greatest of all time by most metrics — 7 World Championships (matching Schumacher), 103 race wins, and 103 pole positions. He also became a cultural icon, championing diversity and speaking on social issues.
2021
The Most Controversial Title Finish Ever
The 2021 season produced the most dramatic finale in F1 history. Max Verstappen (Red Bull) and Lewis Hamilton (Mercedes) entered the final lap of the last race tied on points. A controversial safety car decision gave Verstappen the win and the championship. The FIA later admitted race director Michael Masi had made errors, and Masi was replaced.
2022–24
Verstappen's Reign
With new technical regulations in 2022, Red Bull and Max Verstappen became unstoppable. Verstappen claimed his 4th consecutive World Championship in 2024. But as the 2025 season begins, rivals are closing the gap — making for one of the most anticipated competitive periods in decades.

Chapter 03

The Teams
on the Grid

Ten teams (called "constructors") compete in F1. Each fields two cars with two drivers. Teams design and build their own cars within FIA regulations, though they all share the same engine from one of four suppliers: Mercedes, Ferrari, Renault (Alpine), or Honda (Red Bull Powertrains).

Ferrari
📍 Maranello, Italy · Engine: Ferrari
Lewis Hamilton Charles Leclerc
The most iconic name in motorsport. The Scuderia Ferrari is the only team to have competed in every single Formula One season since 1950. Known as "La Scuderia," their red cars (Rosso Corsa) are the most recognizable in racing. The entire nation of Italy follows them with religious devotion. After a difficult decade, their signing of Lewis Hamilton in 2025 is their biggest move in a generation.
🏆 16 Constructors Championships · 15 Drivers Championships
Red Bull Racing
📍 Milton Keynes, UK · Engine: Honda RBPT
Max Verstappen Sergio Pérez
Founded in 2005 when energy drink company Red Bull bought Jaguar Racing, this team went from newcomers to the most dominant force in modern F1. Under legendary designer Adrian Newey (who left in 2024), they built some of the most technically innovative cars ever seen. Verstappen's four straight titles from 2021–2024 were built on their technical supremacy.
🏆 6 Constructors Championships · 7 Drivers Championships (via Verstappen)
Mercedes-AMG Petronas
📍 Brackley, UK · Engine: Mercedes
George Russell Kimi Antonelli
The team that rewrote the record books. With the introduction of hybrid engines in 2014, Mercedes produced the most dominant stretch of performance in the sport's history — winning 8 consecutive Constructors Championships from 2014–2021. Led by team principal Toto Wolff and powered by Hamilton's genius, they turned F1 into a procession (to some) and a masterclass in engineering (to others).
🏆 8 Constructors Championships · Multiple Drivers Championships
McLaren
📍 Woking, UK · Engine: Mercedes
Lando Norris Oscar Piastri
One of the most storied names in F1. McLaren was the home of Senna and Prost in the 1980s, and Häkkinen and Coulthard in the late 1990s. After a dark decade of underperformance, they've made a remarkable comeback with their young driver pairing of Norris and Piastri. Their iconic papaya orange cars returned to genuine competitiveness in 2023 and they won the 2024 Constructors Championship.
🏆 9 Constructors Championships · 12 Drivers Championships
Aston Martin Aramco
📍 Silverstone, UK · Engine: Mercedes
Fernando Alonso Lance Stroll
Rebranded to Aston Martin in 2021 following billionaire Lawrence Stroll's takeover, this team is investing massively in a state-of-the-art new factory. The signing of 2-time champion Fernando Alonso in 2023 was a major signal of intent. Their British Racing Green cars are among the most beautiful on the grid, and they're building toward a title challenge.
🏆 0 Constructors Championships (as Aston Martin) · growing fast
Haas F1 Team
📍 Kannapolis, USA · Engine: Ferrari
Esteban Ocon Oliver Bearman
The only American-owned team on the grid, Haas joined F1 in 2016. They operate with a unique model — using Ferrari components extensively rather than building everything in-house, making them the most "Ferrari-like" of the independent teams. They made headlines in 2022 when they retained the controversial Nikita Mazepin… then immediately dropped him after Russia invaded Ukraine.
🏆 0 Championships · midfield runners
Kick Sauber (Audi from 2026)
📍 Hinwil, Switzerland · Engine: Ferrari
Nico Hülkenberg Gabriel Bortoleto
One of F1's quiet survivors, this Swiss team has operated under many names (Sauber, BMW Sauber, Alfa Romeo). In 2026 they become the factory Audi team — a huge moment as one of the world's great car brands enters F1 for the first time. Their transformation is one of the most anticipated stories of the next few years.
🏆 0 Championships · becoming the Audi works team in 2026
Williams Racing
📍 Grove, UK · Engine: Mercedes
Alex Albon Carlos Sainz
Frank Williams' team dominated F1 in the late 1980s and 1990s, winning 7 Constructors Championships. Mansell, Prost, Hill, and Villeneuve all won titles with them. After Frank's passing in 2021, the team (now owned by Dorilton Capital) is rebuilding. Williams has a heritage that demands respect — they are British motorsport royalty.
🏆 9 Constructors Championships · 7 Drivers Championships
Alpine F1 Team
📍 Enstone, UK · Engine: Renault
Pierre Gasly Jack Doohan
The rebranded Renault works team, racing under the Alpine brand since 2021. Renault has a rich F1 history — they pioneered the turbocharged engine in the 1970s and won back-to-back championships with Fernando Alonso in 2005–2006. Under the Alpine name they've struggled for consistency but have genuine technical ambitions for the sport's new era.
🏆 2 Constructors Championships (as Renault) · rebuilding
RB (Racing Bulls)
📍 Faenza, Italy · Engine: Honda RBPT

Red Bull's "junior team" — a second F1 entry that serves as a development ground for young drivers. Formerly known as Toro Rosso, then AlphaTauri, and now RB. They've launched the careers of Vettel, Ricciardo, Verstappen, and Gasly. They use the same Honda power unit as Red Bull, making them technically linked to the dominant team.

Chapter 04

Iconic
F1 Circuits

Every track has its own personality. Some are fast and flowing, others a twisting nightmare through city streets. Here are the most significant circuits on the F1 calendar — and why they matter.

01Round
Circuit de Monaco
🇲🇨 Monte Carlo, Monaco
The crown jewel of F1. Racing through the narrow streets of Monaco is the ultimate test of precision — one millimetre from the barriers at 280km/h. The most prestigious race to win, but the least amount of actual overtaking due to the tight layout. Qualifying matters more here than anywhere. Senna won it 6 times. Ayrton famously said: "If you no longer go for a gap that exists, you are no longer a racing driver."
3.337km/lap
02Round
Silverstone Circuit
🇬🇧 Northamptonshire, England
The birthplace of Formula One — the very first World Championship race was held here in 1950. A former RAF airfield transformed into the world's greatest racing circuit. Copse, Maggotts, Chapel, and Beckets — the fast sweeping corners at Silverstone are taken flat-out at full speed and test a driver's nerve like nowhere else. The British Grand Prix crowd is famously electric.
5.891km/lap
03Round
Monza — Temple of Speed
🇮🇹 Monza, Italy
The fastest race on the calendar. Monza's long straights and chicanes produce average speeds of over 260km/h — cars run minimal downforce to maximize straight-line speed. The Italian crowd (Tifosi) turns the grandstands into a wall of red Ferrari flags. When Ferrari wins here — as Leclerc did in 2019 — the celebrations are unlike anything else in sport.
5.793km/lap
04Round
Suzuka International Racing Course
🇯🇵 Suzuka, Japan
Many drivers and engineers consider Suzuka the best circuit in the world. Its figure-of-eight layout, the iconic 130R corner (flat out at 300km/h), the Esses sequence, and Degner — every corner is a masterpiece. Japanese fans are among the most devoted in the world, creating an extraordinary atmosphere. Multiple championships have been decided here, including two infamous Senna-Prost collisions.
5.807km/lap
05Round
Circuit of the Americas (COTA)
🇺🇸 Austin, Texas, USA
F1's American showcase. Opened in 2012 specifically to host the US Grand Prix, COTA was designed to be a modern classic — it borrows elements from great circuits around the world. The steep climb to Turn 1 followed by a fast left-right sweep is one of the most dramatic openers in motorsport. F1's growing popularity in America has made this race one of the biggest events on the calendar.
5.513km/lap
06Round
Spa-Francorchamps
🇧🇪 Stavelot, Belgium
Set in the forests of the Ardennes, Spa is the longest circuit on the calendar and arguably the most challenging. Eau Rouge / Raidillon — a terrifyingly fast uphill corner complex — is the most famous stretch of asphalt in motorsport. The weather changes mid-race. It can be sunny on one part of the circuit and raining on another. Spa produces drama every single year.
7.004km/lap
07Round
Bahrain International Circuit
🇧🇭 Sakhir, Bahrain
The traditional season opener (when not in Australia). Bahrain introduced F1 to the Middle East in 2004 and remains one of the most important venues on the calendar. Under floodlights at night, the desert backdrop creates a unique visual spectacle. The long Tarmac straight and long braking zones into Turn 1 make it a great overtaking circuit.
5.412km/lap
08Round
Las Vegas Street Circuit
🇺🇸 Las Vegas, Nevada, USA
The newest spectacle on the calendar, added in 2023. Racing through the Las Vegas Strip at night past casinos and hotels — past the Bellagio fountains, the MGM Grand — is unlike any other Grand Prix. Fast, long straights, night racing, and the full entertainment package of Las Vegas. It divided opinion initially but has grown quickly into one of the most-watched events globally.
6.201km/lap
Chapter 05

The Machine —
Inside an F1 Car

A Formula One car is arguably the most technologically advanced vehicle ever built for the road. It takes roughly 80,000 components to assemble one. Here's what makes it extraordinary.

⚡
Power Unit (Hybrid Engine)
Not just a regular engine. An F1 "power unit" is a 1.6L V6 turbocharged engine combined with two Motor Generator Units (MGUs) — one harvesting energy from the turbo (MGU-H) and one from braking (MGU-K). Combined output: over 1,000 horsepower. The internal combustion engine alone produces about 620hp — the hybrid systems add the rest. This unit is so complex it took Mercedes a decade to perfect theirs.
🔴
Aerodynamics & Downforce
The most important part of the car you can't see working. At speed, aerodynamic forces press the car into the ground (downforce), allowing it to corner at physically impossible speeds. At 200km/h, an F1 car generates enough downforce to theoretically drive upside down on a ceiling. The front wing, rear wing, and floor are all aerodynamic devices. Teams spend 70% of their development budget on aero.
🎯
DRS (Drag Reduction System)
A movable flap on the rear wing that the driver can open in specific "DRS zones" when following within one second of the car ahead. Opening DRS reduces aerodynamic drag and adds up to 20km/h of straight-line speed, making it easier to overtake. It was introduced in 2011 to increase passing opportunities. Critics say it makes overtaking too easy; fans love the extra action it creates.
🏁
Tires — The Critical Variable
Pirelli is the sole tire supplier. They make 5 dry-weather compounds (C1–C5) plus Intermediate and Full Wet tires. Softer compounds are faster but wear out quickly; harder compounds last longer but are slower. Every race is essentially a puzzle of when to pit and which tires to use. The tire strategy can completely change race results and is one of the most complex and exciting aspects of F1.
🛡️
The Halo — Life Saver
The titanium hoop above the driver's head looks odd but has saved multiple lives. Introduced in 2018 after heavy resistance (many found it ugly), it has since prevented deaths at Silverstone 2018 (Leclerc's crash), Bahrain 2020 (Grosjean's fireball), and most dramatically at Monza 2021 when Verstappen's car landed on Hamilton's head. It can withstand the weight of a London double-decker bus.
🧠
Data Systems & Steering Wheel
An F1 car has over 300 sensors generating 3GB of data per race lap. The steering wheel alone has over 25 buttons, switches, and dials — and costs more than most sports cars. Drivers communicate with engineers constantly via radio. The engineers can monitor every engine parameter, tire temperature, brake temperature, and G-force in real time. It's as much a data exercise as a driving one.
🔥
Brakes
Carbon ceramic brake discs that glow orange-red at over 1,000°C during heavy braking. An F1 car can brake from 300km/h to 0 in under 4 seconds — subjecting the driver to forces of over 5G (5 times their body weight). The braking distances are incredibly short: from 300km/h to a turn entry, you might use just 65 metres. Getting the braking point wrong by a fraction of a second costs places.
⚗️
Materials — Carbon Fibre Everything
The car's "tub" (monocoque) — the central survival cell — is made of carbon fibre, a material 5 times stronger than steel at a quarter of the weight. Almost everything on an F1 car is carbon fibre, including the wings, body panels, and brake ducts. The entire car weighs just 798kg including the driver. Each component is manufactured to tolerances of microns — smaller than a human hair.
Chapter 06

The Race
Weekend

A Grand Prix weekend is a 3-day event (Thursday to Sunday) following a structured format. Understanding the schedule helps you follow what's happening and why each session matters.

Day Session Duration What Happens
Friday Practice 1 (FP1) 60 min Teams gather data about the track — tire degradation, fuel loads, setup options. Drivers learn the circuit. Not important for points but crucial for information gathering. Rookies often get a drive here.
Friday Practice 2 (FP2) 60 min More setup runs. Teams try different aerodynamic and suspension configurations. Often the session where teams start simulating race conditions with high fuel loads.
Saturday Practice 3 (FP3) 60 min Final preparation before qualifying. Teams fine-tune the car setup based on FP1 and FP2 data. The pace starts to build as drivers push closer to the limit.
Saturday Qualifying (Q1/Q2/Q3) 60 min total The most exciting one-lap session in motorsport. Three knockout rounds determine the starting grid. Q1 eliminates the 5 slowest, Q2 eliminates the next 5, Q3 is a final 10-minute shootout for pole position. The pole-sitter leads the grid — a crucial advantage at tight circuits like Monaco.
Sunday Race ~2 hours The main event. Roughly 305km (around 50–70 laps depending on circuit length). Points awarded to top 10 finishers (25-18-15-12-10-8-6-4-2-1). Fastest lap earns 1 bonus point. A race win requires skill, strategy, tire management, and occasionally a bit of luck — safety cars and weather can turn any race on its head.
Sprint Weekends

Some weekends (about 6 per season) include a Sprint Race — a shorter 100km race on Saturday that awards its own points (8-7-6-5-4-3-2-1 for positions 1–8). Sprint weekends have a compressed format: Qualifying moves to Friday evening, Sprint Qualifying (for the sprint grid) on Saturday morning, the Sprint itself on Saturday afternoon, and the full race on Sunday. They divide opinion — fans love the extra action, purists miss the traditional format.

Chapter 07

Rules, Strategy
& How Points Work

F1 has two championships running simultaneously — the Drivers' Championship and the Constructors' Championship. Understanding the scoring and key rules makes watching the races much more rewarding.

Points go to the top 10 finishers: 25 · 18 · 15 · 12 · 10 · 8 · 6 · 4 · 2 · 1, plus 1 bonus point for the fastest lap (if you finish in the top 10). The Drivers' Championship goes to the driver with the most points at the end of the season. The Constructors' Championship adds both drivers' points together — so a team can score up to 50 points per race. Both championships are prestigious; the Constructors' Championship is worth hundreds of millions of dollars in prize money.
Every driver must use at least two different tire compounds during a race (unless it rains). This forces at least one pit stop. A pit stop takes about 2.5 seconds for the fastest crews — 20 mechanics changing all four tires simultaneously. Strategy is everything: some teams "undercut" (pit early to get fresh tires and pass a rival); others "overcut" (stay out longer to get clean air and come out ahead after rivals pit). The strategic battle between teams is often more complex than the on-track battle.
🟢 Green: Track is clear, racing is on.

🟡 Yellow: Hazard ahead — reduce speed, no overtaking in the yellow flag zone.

🔴 Red: Session stopped — major incident or dangerous conditions. Everyone returns to pits.

⚫ Black & White: Warning for unsporting behaviour (like blocking, dirty driving).

⚫ Black: Driver is disqualified — must return to pits immediately.

🏁 Chequered: Session / race is over.

Blue: Shown to a car about to be lapped — must let the faster car past within 3 blue flags or be penalised.
When there's a significant incident, the Safety Car (a Mercedes-AMG GT) enters the track and leads all cars at reduced speed while marshals clear debris or attend to a crash. No overtaking allowed. Crucially, the Safety Car bunches the field together — cars that were laps ahead suddenly have rivals right behind them. This creates dramatic restarts and can completely change who wins. The Virtual Safety Car (VSC) is a milder version — cars slow down but the Safety Car doesn't physically deploy; used for smaller incidents.
Race stewards (officials) can investigate incidents and issue penalties. Common penalties:

5-second penalty: Time added to your race result (for minor infractions like unsafe pit release or minor collisions deemed your fault).
10-second penalty: More serious infractions.
Drive-through penalty: Must drive through the pit lane without stopping — wastes about 25–30 seconds.
Stop-Go penalty: Must stop in pit lane for 10 seconds — very severe.
Grid penalties: Applied before a race for taking extra engine components or for incidents in the previous race.

The stewards' decisions are often controversial and widely debated by fans.
Introduced in 2021, the Cost Cap limits how much teams can spend on car development per year (approximately $135 million USD in 2024, reducing over time). This is designed to prevent the richest teams from simply buying success. Before the cap, Ferrari and Mercedes were spending over $400 million per season. The cap has genuinely improved competitiveness — previously, 3 or 4 teams would fight for wins; now 5 or 6 teams are regularly in contention.
After qualifying, cars enter "Parc Fermé" — a restricted zone where teams can only make limited changes to the car before the race. This prevents teams from completely rebuilding their cars overnight with different specifications. It means the qualifying setup is essentially what you race with. Any significant changes (like changing the front wing angle) require FIA permission. It keeps the competition fair and prevents wealthier teams from simply throwing resources at overnight rebuilds.
Chapter 08

F1 Glossary —
The Language of the Sport

F1 has its own language. Understanding these terms will help you follow broadcasts, commentary, and conversations with other fans.

Apex
The innermost point of a corner — the ideal point where a driver clips the inside to carry maximum speed through the turn.
Backmarker
A slower car (typically at the back of the field) that the race leaders are about to lap. Also called a "lapped car."
Box, Box, Box
The radio message from an engineer telling the driver to come into the pit lane. ("Box" = pit box.)
Clean Air
Racing without a car directly in front. Aerodynamically, following another car disturbs the airflow, costing downforce and lap time. Leading in "clean air" is a significant advantage.
Constructor
The official term for an F1 team — used because teams "construct" their own cars. The Constructors' Championship is the team title.
Dirty Air
The turbulent air behind another car that reduces downforce on the following car, making it harder to follow closely and overtake. A big problem in F1 historically.
DNF
Did Not Finish — a retirement from the race due to mechanical failure, accident, or disqualification.
Double Stack
When a team brings both cars into the pit lane simultaneously. Rare but tactically powerful — saves time overall but risky if one car is delayed.
Downforce
The aerodynamic force pushing the car into the ground at speed. More downforce = faster through corners, but more drag = slower on straights. Finding the balance is central to setup.
DRS
Drag Reduction System. A movable rear wing flap that reduces drag on straights, boosting top speed by up to 20km/h. Only usable in designated zones and only within 1 second of the car ahead.
ERS
Energy Recovery System. The hybrid part of the power unit that harvests electrical energy from braking and the turbocharger, storing it for deployment as extra power.
Formation Lap
The lap before the race starts, where cars form up on the grid. Drivers weave to warm their tires. After this lap, they take their grid positions and wait for the 5 red lights to start the race.
FIA
Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile — the governing body of F1. They set the rules, approve circuits, license drivers, and adjudicate incidents.
Graining
When thin slivers of rubber shear off the tire surface and stick to it, reducing grip. A temporary phenomenon that often clears after a few laps as the grains wear away.
Intermediate Tires
The "middle" tire for damp but not soaking conditions. They work when there's light rain or a drying wet track. Called "inters" by teams and commentators.
Locking Up
When a driver brakes so hard the wheels stop rotating and the car slides — causing flat spots on the tires and loss of control. Usually followed by a cloud of tire smoke.
Marbles
Bits of rubber that come off tires and accumulate off the racing line. Driving on marbles is treacherous — like driving on ball bearings. Cars often go "off line" when overtaking and lose grip.
Overcut
Staying out on track longer than the car ahead before pitting, hoping to come out ahead after they've pitted first. Works when fresh tires don't offer a big enough advantage.
Oversteer
When the rear of the car slides out in a corner — the car rotates more than intended. Can be spectacular when controlled. Also called "snap oversteer" when it happens suddenly.
Parc Fermé
A "closed park" where cars are kept after qualifying with very limited adjustments allowed before the race. From French — meaning "enclosed park."
Podium
Finishing in the top 3 positions. The "podium" is the raised platform where the top 3 drivers stand for the trophy ceremony and national anthems — one of F1's most iconic images.
Pole Position
Starting at the front of the grid — the reward for the fastest qualifying lap. Starting from pole is a massive advantage, especially on tight circuits where overtaking is difficult.
Power Unit
The complete engine system in F1: the internal combustion engine + turbocharger + two electric motor-generator units. Called a "power unit" rather than just "engine" to reflect this complexity.
Slipstream
Driving closely behind another car in their "tow" — the low-pressure air pocket behind the car reduces aerodynamic resistance, boosting top speed. Common in qualifying laps and on long straights.
Tifosi
The passionate Italian fans of Scuderia Ferrari. The name means "fans" or "enthusiasts" in Italian. The Tifosi at Monza create one of the most extraordinary atmospheres in all of sport.
Understeer
When the front of the car doesn't turn as much as the driver steers — the car "ploughs" forward instead of turning. Often called "pushing" — the front tires lose grip and the car runs wide.
Undercut
Pitting earlier than the car ahead to get fresh tires and post faster lap times, hoping to come out ahead when they eventually pit. One of the most common race-changing strategies.
Wall of Champions
A notorious wall at the Circuit Gilles Villeneuve in Montreal that has collected World Champions over the years (Hill, Schumacher, Villeneuve all crashed here). One of F1's beloved running stories.
Works Team
A team that is officially supported or owned by an engine manufacturer (e.g., Ferrari, Mercedes, Red Bull/Honda). They get the best, most up-to-date engines. Customer teams buy last year's engines.
